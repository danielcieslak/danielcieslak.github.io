Mean vs. Median

Which “average” should you actually trust?

“What’s the average?” sounds like one question, but it hides two very different answers. The two most common are the mean and the median, and knowing which one to reach for is one of the highest-leverage habits in data work.

The mean

The mean is the arithmetic average: add every value and divide by how many there are.

mean = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) / n

For the salaries 30, 32, 35, 38, 40 (in thousands), the mean is 175 / 5 = 35. So far, so intuitive.

The median

The median is the middle value once the data is sorted. With an even count, it’s the average of the two middle values. For the same salaries, the median is also 35 — the two agree when the data is roughly symmetric.

Where they disagree

Now add one executive earning 500k to that team:

DataMeanMedian
30, 32, 35, 38, 403535
30, 32, 35, 38, 40, 500112.536.5

One extreme value dragged the mean from 35 to 112.5 — a number that describes nobody in the room. The median barely moved. That’s the whole point: the mean is sensitive to outliers; the median is robust to them.

A rule of thumb

Use the median when the data is skewed or has outliers — incomes, house prices, response times, anything with a long tail. Use the mean when the data is roughly symmetric and every value should carry equal weight, or when you need a quantity you can add up (like a total budget).

Better yet, report both. When the mean and median are far apart, that gap is itself the story: it’s telling you the distribution is skewed, and that a single “average” was never going to be honest on its own.
